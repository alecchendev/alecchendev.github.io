title: A Belief to Unlearn
---

I think there is this implicit belief that I've held, and that many people of my generation and many people in America or other affluent societies hold, that lobotomizes human flourishing. It's that *things will be fine and you are inconsequential*.

What is the importance of this?

One central theme in my life is the cycle of desire, success/failure, and growth. You want something, you get it or you don't, and you learn from that experience. It's critical that it's a cycle, because it means you ante up again, over and over. When you die, you stop the cycle. When you stop the cycle, you die.

This doesn't mean you need to be in a constant state of anguish, consumed by desperation in pursuit of achievement. It doesn't expel all comfort. But it means you are in motion. You have wants. You take action to move toward them. And something happens. Things change. You change.

This is so ubiquitous that you even see this cycle in [how stories are structured](https://channel101.fandom.com/wiki/Story_Structure_101:_Super_Basic_Shit).

This cycle is our nature, and it's how we end up with incredible artifacts of human ingenuity. It's how people you look up to became the people they are and did the things that you admire. It's how we can fly in airplanes and look down at the earth and see ridiculous, massive swaths of land cultivated for human civilization. It's how my neighbor grew to have her sincerity and warmth.

People have different ways of defining happiness, success, fulfillment—whatever you want to call a life well lived. But I think there are universal truths among almost all definitions. It's one where someone grows. A person experiences this cycle over and over without giving up. Whether they fail or succeed, they ante up again.

Complacency nukes this cycle. It reduces the amplitude of the cycle and extends the wavelength, but it does not give you more time. This belief that things will be fine and that you are inconsequential embodies complacency.

I think this is one of the things I could improve on the most. I wouldn't say I'm a particularly complacent person, but greater magnitudes of urgency make such a big difference, and I'm currently capturing such a small sliver of the whole. I believe that my greatest limiting factor right now is urgency.

How do you cultivate a sense of urgency?

I've observed many different reasons for my own and others' senses of urgency.

Some people's sense of urgency comes from ego. They need to do something, make it succeed, or make it happen faster because they need to be *somebody*. In many cases, it comes from some attachment to their identity in some form, whether their identity surrounds their passion for a mission or some domain, or the need to make money, prove to people or themselves that they're capable, or even just be interesting. Having too big of an ego is annoying and ultimately detrimental, but cultivating an identity is a skill that can be [harnessed effectively](https://x.com/justinskycak/status/2085525832307720684).

But where does the identity you or I seek come from? It seems like some combination of cultural exposure and the random seed you were given by God. Both of these feel arbitrary. There's some feeling inside me that needs to have a reason for things and is conflicted by this arbitrariness. But people don't really live their lives *because* of reasons. People's life choices are usually explained *in retrospect* by reasons. We may make individual choices based on certain reasons, but the overarching trajectory is often dominated by the culture and the seed.

So what then? I want to understand for myself how to create a sense of urgency. I had this assumption that there might be some reason out there that would somehow unlock an intrinsic sense of urgency within me. But I think that assumption is wrong, and the question it's asking is not even the right one.

They say [action precedes motivation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W3I3kAg2J7w). Action → results → motivation is a sort of alternate, parallel cycle to the one I describe above. I recognized that this is true for building routines and skills, but I think this also describes how we form beliefs. Over a long period of time, actions may become more than just something we do: they may become something more intrinsic, a part of our worldview and identity, i.e. a belief. Beliefs are not formed because we found some specific reason, but from a long history of experiences, knowledge, and feedback from the giant control system of life. And what I am really after is changing a belief. Although I've made things out to sound rather deterministic, I believe we have agency over our actions.

Actions are the root. Nothing precedes action. To change this belief, I must change my actions.

[Get busy living, or get busy dying.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Shawshank_Redemption)
