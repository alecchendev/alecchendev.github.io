I just finished rereading flowers for algernon. Writing some thoughts.

I find myself really enjoying stories about people with disabilities and great loss. This book, one flew over the cuckoos nest, and movies like sound of metal, shawshank redemption. Stories of outsiders, like her. I wonder why. Maybe it's relaxing to imagine life with less expectations. It makes me more grateful for what I have I think. It also just gives me perspective. Life is pretty rough for many people, and it's interesting to see people navigate situations arguably more [difficult than my own](https://x.com/dhh/status/2120109028646801866). Sometimes I feel like an outsider myself and it is cathartic to relate to these characters.

There are many references in this story. Some off the top of my mind is the story at the end with mistaking windmills for dragons. There's a couple references to plato, about the light and the cave, as well as the message at the beginning of the book. Would like to understand these more.

In a way charlie just speed ran life. We all start out as children with little intelligence and grow to become more intelligent, to varying degrees, and we all descend back down to senility or death, again to varying degrees. The way he experiences these changes are unique to him in some ways, but also very common. We all form egos when we become smarter. And "smart"-er people, especially ones with fewer friends (by cause or effect), can become arrogant or apathetic to others. We have much simpler/easier goals early on and look at adults with great admiration and think they know everything. And we eventually find out they don't, though like bert said, they don't necessarily claim to. And eventually we all descend back to dust. I haven't really gotten to the dust-descent part of life yet, but I can imagine people deal with impending death/decline in many of the ways charlie did. Grasping on to what's remaining, depression, denial, acceptance.

[Dan harmon story circle](https://channel101.fandom.com/wiki/Story_Structure_101:_Super_Basic_Shit) coming into play here. Charlie got what he wanted, but at a cost, and returned to his original place, having changed.

It's sad how smart charlie was such a prick. It's understandable, that realizing some hard truths can make you cynical. And he did have a pretty rough life. Though we did see him change a bit. He became a bit more forgiving over time, but still he held a hard temper. Is this to say intelligence makes you an asshole? That question is maybe not the interesting one. Maybe just that it takes extra effort to be kind as a "smart" person. To recognize the darkness of the world but to still forgive and spread light.

Just realized he said to norma he'd come back and help, but that was basically the last we heard of them. Wouldn't norma have come looking for him?

It was interesting hearing norma's side of the story. Their mother was right about norma's life being negatively impacted by being the sibling of "someone like charlie." But she made it out alright and was able to distinguish what was his fault vs. circumstance/parents. That's a hard thing to do.

Somehow even though smart charlie was more mean, it was still so sad to see him descend to his former state. Like if you don't like someone, you'd expect to feel more of a "he deserved what he got" attitude. But the characters in the bakery even changed when they saw him and heard what happened. He lost it all. And importantly, when he was back to how he was originally, he still had his kindness.

Algernon's erratic behavior is interesting. I know it's meant to act as foreshadowing, and I guess Charlie sorta experiences that. He becomes more hostile to those around him as he descended. He even did the thing where he laid around depressed for some time. Charlie didn't die at least though.

The thing about the [rorschach test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rorschach_test) is interesting. I forgot that the test is not about what you see, but other attributes of your reactions. I wonder what the test would say about me, but I guess it's sorta too late now lol. Hopefully I can forget again and take it one day.

The emphasis on love and sex. I liked how as one of the final things he could recognize as he declined was his gratefulness for his love with alice. The whole sexual repression and emotional immaturity piece was interesting. I don't remember that playing as big of a role when I first read it. I felt the romance between alice and him was a little bit odd. At one point she was his teacher and he was a child, and he was just a good student, maybe one of her favorites. But it seemed kinda abrupt that once he became kinda smart they were both ready to start smooching. I wouldn't have thought about the concepts of sex and emotional maturity when hearing the premise of this book. But it's a good point and added depth.

Charlie's positive attitude towards learning was one of his most admirable attributes, and is basically what initiates this whole story. It's interesting that it sort of has a dark motivation, from his mom beating him for not learning, being ashamed of him and sending him away, and charlie wanting to be liked. But when you see charlie wanting to learn, even after knowing that, it still seems so innocent. Like his desire doesn't feel like it's coming from a place of insecurity, it just seems like something he wants just 'cause.

The window metaphor. The reference back to it at the end was spooky. Not exactly sure what it means. Maybe how technically everything in life was in front of charlie, and he could see it, but he couldn't break through. Like a glass ceiling. It's interesting "charlie" referred to the original charlie, whereas I'm not sure we really had a name for smart charlie. It was always "you" "i" "he". The split-up between charlie's two selves was very clear. They were really like two different people. I felt the transition to being smarter felt kinda fast, at least in how the writing changed. And in a way that sort of makes sense, since the two characters are so different. Hmm, online I see things about the window being between the charlies. Smart charlie is intellectually gifted, but lacks intimate connection, while the old charlie is the opposite. The window is the barrier between each other. And only as charlie transitions to his old self is he able to truly connect with alice. At that point he's narrowly standing on the line between them.

Re-reading the plato passage at the beginning. I never really knew the full allegory of the cave, and still don't, but re-reading a synopsis it's really fitting here. Prisoners of the cave only see shadows of people and events. They study and worship representations of reality, without knowledge of reality itself. A prisoner is freed and leaves the cave, being blinded by the light, but seeing reality. He comes back to tell the others, and now cannot see the shadows as clearly, his eyes having adjusted to the light. The prisoners think he's lost it, and turn against him. This is exactly what happens to charlie at the bakery. Not that he's trying to free them, but his journey into the light takes away his ability for connection, and he's isolated. But eventually he still tries to give back via his research, which apparently plato had talked about, i.e. governing/educating/truth-telling has a social cost but is still important. That's the allegory, but the passage itself is referring to something specific:

> Any one who has common sense will remember that the bewilderments of the eyes are of two kinds, and arise from two causes, either from coming out of the light or from going into the light, which is true of the mind's eye, quite as much as of the bodily eye; and he who remembers this when he sees any one whose vision is perplexed and weak, will not be too ready to laugh; he will first ask whether that soul of man has come out of the brighter life, and is unable to see because unaccustomed to the dark, or having turned from darkness to the day is dazzled by excess of light. And he will count the one happy in his condition and state of being, and he will pity the other; or, if he have a mind to laugh at the soul which comes from below into the light, there will be more reason in this than in the laugh which greets him who returns from above out of the light into the den.

There's more reason in laughing at the one entering the light than the one returning from it. In plato's case, that's returning to help others after being educated. In charlie's case, that's true too, but also in his decline, his friends at the bakery really are his friends. They don't laugh, they stick up for him. That being said, those at the bakery were more like ones tending the fire in the cave, while charlie was the prisoner. They only laughed at him when they were beyond him, but eventually he went beyond them, outside the cave and into the light. But he lost the "warmth" of the fire, the personal connection. Very interesting how the author used "warmth" quite a bit in describing old charlie's demeanor.

When charlie becomes smart, he gets disoriented. He enjoys learning and for some time that's satisfying in itself. But eventually he's sort of purposeless and isolated. But at a point, he comes to want to give back to people that struggled like him. I thought that was a good message. The pursuit of knowledge purely is maybe less valuable than directing it towards something helpful. To serve the city after becoming educated. [To open the doors to the deathless.](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN6_1.html)
