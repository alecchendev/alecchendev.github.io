- I thought of a way to increase my power *and* delete the bumpers entirely. If I make my launcher into more of a cross bow shape with two springs pulling on the sides, I don't have a front anchor, the sled can fly freely (off the rails if needed), and the springs will just pull it back so it doesn't fly off completely. This would mean I'd use two springs which might help me get more power as well. I can mount the side prongs from the bottom of the aluminum extrusion so it doesn't interfere with the sled path. This might also allow me to not buy a very specific spring. It's not strictly necessary, but it's nice if my designs can be simpler to reproduce (though the specific spring is probably not a real issue in this regard).
- I think I should probably try out this design, but first I want to just gauge how much power/velocity I should be expecting from my system, in case I'd want to get a specific/stronger spring.
	- I measured the spring constants, and for the spring I'm using, it's 1.368 N/cm (in my drawing I put N/mm which was wrong), or 136.8 N/m.
	- I measured the distance that the tip of the spring attached to the sled is moving is 13.5 cm, and the spring starts from just barely stretched.
	- Simplifying/underestimating by assuming the spring is basically starting from relaxed, the work done by the spring should be 1/2\*kx^2. Plugging in 136.8 for k and 0.135 for x -> 1/2\*(136.8)(0.135)^2 = 1.25 J.
	- Theoretically, with my glider of weight 90g, that work should translate to a certain velocity. 1.25 = 1/2mv^2. m = 0.09 -> v = 5.27 m/s. That seems a lot faster than the speed I'm observing. Realistically the sled and launch mount also add 20 grams so -> v = 4.78 m/s, still fast.
	- There's definitely friction between my sled and the aluminum, but I wonder how much. When I peripherally toss the sled around on the rail, it slides really smoothly. I'd be sort of surprised if this was contributing a meaningful proportion of energy loss. Though, my glider might add more friction since it weighs more. I do have sides of the sled hugging the rail, but they aren't pressed against each other. Dry PLA on aluminum's friction coefficient is 0.25-0.35. Let's over estimate with 0.35. Glider + mount + sled is 110 g = 0.11 kg. Distance up the sled is 0.135 m. So force from friction is friction_coefficient \* mass \* gravity \* cos(10) = 0.35 \* 0.11 \* 9.8 \* cos(10) = 0.37 N. Sustained across the length of the track -> 0.37 N \* 0.135 m = 0.05 J. Doesn't seem like much of a dent, ~4%. Maybe my spring is just not as strong as I think.
	- I recalculated, and I got a spring constant of 111.23 N/m. Clearly my measurement is not super precise, but also my spring has stretched a little which could have a big impact. But even then that still leaves me with 1.01 J which translates to 4.29 m/s which is much more than I'm seeing.
	- The spring itself is 4g. Seems negligible.
	- Other things it could be: drag from the aircraft (probably not), friction from other contact between sled and aluminum if it's being tilted as it's pulled (harder to get a feel for, though I'd imagine the weight of the plane would hold this down somewhat..), my perception of the speed is off.
	- I would really like to understand what about my model here is not representative of reality, but in the meantime I'm going to give my cross bow design a shot.
