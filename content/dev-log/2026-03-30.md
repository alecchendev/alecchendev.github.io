- why does a curving stream of air have lower pressure on the inside of the curve?
	- seems to be a couple elements at play. "stickiness" of air to a surface + centripetal acceleration, increased velocity.
	- first, the simplest most extreme case is a flat wing at an incline. air travelling horizontally will just hit more of the bottom side than the top side. air curves around the top side (since otherwise it'd create a void), but intuitively less air than would have if it wasn't at any incline. makes sense that there's more pressure on the bottom than top, and so there's a force upwards.
	- the curve is interesting. there's something called the [coanda effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coand%C4%83_effect). here's a [great simple youtube explainer](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NvzXKZNJ7ZU). it seems that air "sticks" to surfaces, i.e. the flow follows the contour of a surface, because there's friction acting between air molecules. with air moving horizontally, the air present near the surface gets dragged with the moving air, causing a pressure difference, then more incoming air swoops in to make up the pressure difference. it would be really cool to see a visual proving this theory, where there should be a delay from the initial air flow.
